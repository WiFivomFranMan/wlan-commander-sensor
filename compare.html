Two sniffers,four captures

A WLANPi Go and a NanoPi Zero2 with Osprey antennas, each swept twice — once free-standing, once held in a hand. Both ran the same Intel BE200 on the same channel plan, so the difference is antennas and enclosure. The second capture of each pair is what makes the hand separable from the hardware.

The Go loses more, and loses most where it can least afford to

A hand costs the Go +2.1 dB overall against +1.3 dB for the NanoPi, and on 6 GHz the gap is starkest: +5.0 dB against +1.4 dB. The Osprey antennas sit proud of the case on their own connectors; the Go's are inside the shell, under the palm.

This also explains the earlier, dirtier run. Held, the NanoPi's 2.4 GHz lead reads +3.6 dB; free-standing it settles to +2.1 dB. Roughly a third of what looked like an antenna advantage was the hand.

How the numbers were made

method

Beacons only, p95 of RSSI in dBm. Access points are paired by BSSID and frequency, never by name, because multi-BSSID radios reuse one SSID across bands.

Each BSSID gets a home frequency — the modal one across all four captures, so every sweep is cut identically — and beacons heard while the radio was tuned elsewhere are discarded. A pair needs 5+ beacons on both sides to appear. Hidden and malformed SSIDs are excluded.

All four captures are single-radio and tri-band: one interface block each, roughly 50 frequencies, reaching 7095 MHz. That was checked rather than assumed — an earlier comparison of these two devices was wrong because the Go had split its sweep across a BE200 and a built-in Broadcom, and pooling the two compared a NanoPi BE200 against a Broadcom on a third of the channels.
